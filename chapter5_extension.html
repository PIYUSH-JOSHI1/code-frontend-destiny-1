5.1.1 The Court Behind the Hostel

I did not choose badminton; the court was simply on the way to everything. It stood behind our hostel, a concrete rectangle with faded lines, one net that sagged in the middle like a tired hammock, and floodlights that worked on approximately seventy percent of the days you needed them. Nobody ever announced its existence. You simply heard it every evening — the hollow knock of a shuttle on wood, and laughter that carried up to the third-floor windows.

One evening my roommate gave me a choice with no options in it: come and watch, or sit alone in the room. I went to watch. Within ten minutes a senior had thrown me a racket, and within ten more the shuttle had been everywhere on that court except the place I aimed at. I apologised twice, called the racket defective once, and was laughed at honestly and completely. That was my first match: me versus my own grip, and I lost in straight games.

But something had already happened that evening that I did not have vocabulary for yet. For ninety minutes my head had gone completely quiet — no submissions, no results, no future — only a small white object arriving at speeds my whole attention had to meet. I walked back to the room sweating and strangely light. I did not know it then, but I had just been introduced to the cheapest therapy a student can buy.

5.1.2 The Six O’Clock Religion

The court ran on an unwritten constitution that no warden ever drafted. Games started at six, ended at seven-thirty, and the winner stayed on while the loser’s side rotated out — a system that produced both fierce rallies and sudden, democratic retirements. Court booking happened by handshake, disputes were settled by a replay, and the man who brought the shuttlecock held seniority no committee could overrule.

What fascinated me most was the mixture of people the net collected. Toppers and backbenchers, seniors and juniors, the gym regulars and the first-timers — all of them equal at exactly twenty-one points. The court asked nobody their percentage and checked nobody’s attendance. On that concrete, a university topper and a backbencher were simply two people arguing about whether the shuttle had touched the line, which, by ancient law, it had not.

Within a month, six o’clock had become a religion I did not have to believe in — only attend. That, I later understood, was the whole trick: a fixed slot does the work motivation cannot. Motivation asks every evening whether you feel like playing. A slot does not ask. The court met at six, and I either kept my appointment with it or I did not, and for once in my student life, I mostly did.

5.1.3 The Senior Who Fixed My Grip

I played badly for a long time and proudly blamed the racket, the lights, the wind inside a closed hall. Then one evening the tallest senior in our hostel stopped the game mid-rally, walked over, and rearranged my fingers on the grip like a man adjusting a camera he had owned for years. You are arm-wrestling the shuttle, he said. The wrist gives it life; the arm only delivers it.

That ten-minute correction did what months of effort had failed to do. My smashes stopped apologising on arrival, my clears finally cleared, and my wrist learnt a vocabulary my arm had been faking. He watched me play one more game, nodded like a teacher signing an assignment, and went back to his own match without another word. I practised that grip for the rest of the year like it was a formula I had smuggled out of an exam hall.

That correction became one of the defining lessons of my education, and it had nothing to do with badminton. A senior’s ten minutes saved me a year of wrong practice — the exact exchange rate I later found in code, where a mentor’s one comment saved me days of confident wrongness. Ask early, I decided that evening. Pride costs ten hours; a question costs ten minutes.

5.1.4 Sweat Before Supper

The changes arrived quietly, the way good code refactors arrive — no announcements, just things working better. My appetite, which had run on chaos, fixed its own schedule. Sleep stopped being a negotiation and became a collapse of the pleasant kind. And mess food, the same mess food I had criticised all first year, suddenly tasted earned. It turns out dinner after a real rally is a different dish from dinner after a nap.

The strangest benefit was where it showed up in my work. The best ideas of that year did not arrive while I scrolled; they arrived mid-rally, between points, in the gaps where the mind was too busy to interrupt itself. I would walk off the court with a solution to a bug that had resisted two hours of staring. Sweat, I discovered, is an excellent compiler for thoughts that refuse to build.

Years later, in a hackathon chapter I have already written, I called stamina a design decision. What I did not mention there is where that belief was first compiled: on this court, in these evenings, one exhausted rally at a time. The body, I learnt long before any judge confirmed it, is not a transport arrangement for the brain. It is the machine the brain runs on — and machines that never move, rust.

5.1.5 The Inter-Department Tournament

Every February the college held its inter-department badminton tournament, and in my second year our circle finally entered with more courage than credentials. My doubles partner was my roommate — a boy whose serve had a mind of its own and whose smash was legally classified as a lob. We practised for two weeks, designed one signal system of blinks and nods, and called our partnership strategy, which flattered it considerably.

The draw gave us the mechanical department first, and nobody expected anything from us, which is the most dangerous thing a team can be given. We played above ourselves, took the first game on pure noise from the sideline, and eventually scraped through. The quarter-final, though, met the eventual champions, and our fairytale ended the way most do — 21-19 in the third game, with my last smash landing a centimetre wide and a whole department exhaling at once.

Here is the part I remember more than the score: we applauded the winners, and then we ate together. That is what sport teaches that examinations never do — losing in front of people and shaking their hands in the same hour. I had already begun collecting a syllabus of losing in hackathons; the court had been teaching the same course all along, at a cheaper fee and with better exercise attached.

5.1.6 The Cheapest Therapy in Town

Every student discovers exam anxiety, and most discover it alone, staring at a ceiling at two in the morning. I discovered a loophole. After any bad paper, I stopped brooding in the room and went to the court instead, and ninety minutes later the same problem sat in my head, smaller, wearing its actual size. Sweat turned out to be an excellent solvent for thoughts that will not dissolve otherwise.

The court was also the only place in college where nobody’s history followed them. Not once in three years did anyone ask my marks there. The topper’s serve got returned the same way the backbencher’s did, and the net kept its opinions entirely sporting. For a student brain that spent all day being measured, that concrete rectangle was a small country with no currency except the rally.

So I will say it plainly, because nobody said it to me early enough: everyone needs a court. A place where the mind’s forty tabs close at once, where the body does the talking and the head is only allowed to watch. It does not have to be badminton. It only has to be regular, physical and slightly out of breath. The habit this whole chapter is built on began with one roommate dragging me outside — and the friends he introduced me to there are the next pages of this chapter.

5.2.1 The Mess Table Parliament

Every friend circle has an address, and ours was the third table from the fan in the mess. Nobody elected it, nobody minuted it, yet by the second month of college the parliament convened there at eight o’clock every night with the reliability of a government that actually works. Seats had owners. The corner seat belonged to whoever arrived first, and the chair nearest the fan was defended with the seriousness of a border dispute.

The parliament had its permanent members. There was the topper who explained the day’s lecture while eating, as if digestion and differentiation could share a brain. There was the food critic, who reviewed every dal like a newspaper restaurant column. There was the quiet one who said little and noticed everything, and there was the comedian, whose one job — executed with professional consistency — was to make the table forget whatever paper had gone badly that day.

Years later, when people spoke about networking and communities, I always thought of that table first. A friend circle is not an accident of proximity; it is a system. The mess table was where the day got debugged out loud, where a bad test became a joke and a good day became a story. Before any habit of this chapter made me healthier, that table already knew the first rule of a healthy mind: you cannot carry a day alone.

5.2.2 The Economics of Borrowing

Hostel friendship is measured in units the bank would not recognise: chargers, notes, an iron, one plate of whatever arrived from somebody’s home. The circle ran a parallel economy where the currency was small favours, and the ledger — though nobody wrote it — balanced itself over months with better accuracy than any accounting software I have used since.

Submission week showed the system at full load. One laptop circulated between four boys like a government job; a hotspot was donated for a night and returned with gratitude; the boy with the printer access became, for two days, the most visited man in the wing. Nobody kept formal score, and yet everybody remembered. When my turn came to be the charger or the notes or the two a.m. proof-reader, I paid without being asked twice.

That economy taught me what friendship actually deposits. It is not the grand gestures films advertise; it is a hundred tiny transfers that compound quietly over years. Never keep the score loudly, but never stop keeping it honestly — because the day you need a large withdrawal, it is the small deposits that the whole circle remembers. The best banks I ever knew had mess-table branches.

5.2.3 The Two O’Clock Conversation

Deadline nights had a second shift that nobody scheduled: the two o’clock conversation. When the laptops finally closed and the corridor went quiet, four boys would drift to the stairs or the terrace, and the talk would drift somewhere the day’s jokes never reached. Placements. Family expectations. The private fear that everyone else was somehow ahead. The hostel’s official volume was noise; its real business was conducted in these whispers.

One such night, a friend of ours — the funny one, the table’s comedian — said quietly that he sometimes felt invisible, that his marks were average and his jokes were the only thing anyone noticed. Nobody offered solutions; at two in the morning, solutions are premature. We simply stayed, and argued his case back to him, listing what we actually saw when we looked at him. By three, the boy who made everyone laugh was laughing too, and it was not a performance.

I have never forgotten that night, because it taught me the cheapest mental-health technology ever invented: a friend who stays. A problem spoken aloud loses weight; that is not philosophy, it is physics of a kind. Every mind needs a two o’clock friend, and every friend must occasionally be the two o’clock. Talking, we discovered, is maintenance — and maintenance, as I keep learning in this chapter, is part of the work.

5.2.4 The Friend Who Asked Twice

Among all the habits my friends taught me, the rarest was the art of checking in. Most people ask “are you okay?” the way they ask for attendance — ritually, expecting the printed reply. One friend in our circle had trained himself to ask it twice. The first “are you okay?” collects the automatic fine. The second, asked an hour later with actual eyes, is the one that collects the truth.

Result week tested everyone (my own result panic deserves its own chapter, and the book has given it one). That week my answers were monosyllables and my chai went cold untouched, and this friend did not lecture me about attitude. He simply appeared, said the mess was serving something non-vegetarian — a sentence that has saved more students than any counselling cell — and sat with me until I talked. The real answer arrived on the second asking. It always does.

Since then I try to be that friend for others, with mixed early results and improving technique. Asking twice is a skill, and answering honestly is a bigger one. Between them stands the whole subject of this chapter — a healthier mind is not built alone in a room; it is co-built, in small conversations, by people who refuse to accept the first fine as the truth.

5.2.4115

5.2.5 The Circle That Edited Me

Friends, I eventually understood, are code reviewers for your character. They catch your bad habits in review — the rising temper, the comfortable excuses, the small dishonesties nobody else is close enough to notice — before those habits reach production and embarrass you in front of strangers. A good circle does not clap at everything. A good circle comments on the pull request.

After our national hackathon win, when a newspaper clipping had begun to circulate and my sentences had begun to grow adjectives, one friend delivered a review I still keep. He waited for the right moment, then said: you were the same boy who could not find teammates in first year, so stay findable. One sentence. My arrogance never fully recovered, which was the entire intention. A circle that only praises is a linter with the rules switched off.

This is also how you choose a circle, if you are choosing. Audit it the way you audit dependencies: keep the ones that make your builds better, remove the ones that only inflate the bundle. Some friendships are loud cheerleaders and some are quiet compilers; the second kind hurts briefly and saves you for years. My circle edited me for four years, and I have been grateful in every draft since.

5.2.6 Scattered Cities, One Group Chat

Graduation did what graduations do: it took the mess table and distributed it across six cities and three time zones. The parliament dissolved, and something smaller but stubborn survived in its place — a group chat that has now outlived several phones. The seat nearest the fan has become whoever replies first. The food critic still reviews restaurants by photograph. The comedian’s jokes now arrive one hour late from a different timezone, which somehow makes them funnier.

Distance tested the circle the way only distance can, and the results surprised me. The bonds I assumed were built on proximity turned out to be built on attention: birthday calls that cross timezones, one annual reunion where everybody pretends not to have aged, and the quiet rule that a message left unread for a week is still answered, never buried. Rituals, I learnt, are the load-bearing walls of friendship. Remove them and the roof sags, wherever everyone lives.

Friendship, it turns out, obeys the same law as every habit in this chapter: it survives on small, regular deposits, and dies of large, long silences. A text, a call, a remembered detail — these are the ink crosses of the group chat, one per day. The people who built me still refill me from six cities away. That is the next habit’s perfect introduction, because the mind this chapter protects was never a solo project.

5.3.1 The First Hour Rule

I discovered a quiet truth by accident, the way most discoveries actually happen: the first hour of the morning decides the whole day. For one month I did a small experiment on myself, nothing scientific — just a line in a diary every night about how the day had gone. When I read the lines together, the pattern was embarrassing in its clarity. The day’s character was almost always visible in its opening hour.

If the phone won that hour, the day played defence — messages answered late, lectures attended in spirit, every task arriving already overdue. If I won it — water, a few stretches, ten minutes of silence — the day played on my terms, and even its accidents behaved like plot twists instead of disasters. Same hostel, same syllabus, same boy; only the first hour was different.

There is no magic trick here, and I want to be honest about that. No five a.m. cult, no ice bath, no quote on the wall. Just order, repeated until it stops feeling like effort and starts feeling like character. The first hour is the smallest unit of the day and, therefore, the most powerful one. Whoever owns it, owns the direction of everything that follows.

5.3.2 The Alarm Across the Room

Waking early in a hostel sounds like a joke told by someone who has never lived in one. Doors slam on different schedules, alarms cry in four different voices, and one friend snores in surround sound. Willpower, I learnt quickly, has no chance in that acoustics. So I stopped trusting willpower and built a small system instead — because systems, unlike intentions, survive the room they live in.

Rule one: the alarm lives across the room, so standing up is compulsory. For the first week this felt like cruelty; my morning self developed entire legal arguments about why the bed needed me. My roommate and I signed a silent treaty — if either alarm was silenced by the other, the sleeper owed one samosa. The Cold War of the snooze button ended in a week, and the treaty still stands in my memory as my first successful international agreement.

The lesson inside the comedy is one I use in every habit of this chapter: design beats willpower. Do not try to become the kind of person who resists the snooze button; become the kind of person whose alarm is across the room. Move the obstacle, not the intention. An engineer fixes a buggy system by changing the environment, and a morning is just a system that needs its first fix.

5.3.3 Water Before the Phone

Rule two of the system sounds too small to matter and matters the most: the water bottle sits on the table before the phone does, and the first thing that enters my mouth each day is water, not notifications. It began as a health tip from a fitness-obsessed senior and survived as something deeper — a way of telling my own brain who this day belongs to.

Then the bigger rule: screens stay off for the first thirty minutes. No negotiations, no exceptions, not even for the group chat’s emergency, which in three years of monitoring has never once been an actual emergency. Those thirty minutes are filled with the least impressive activities ever advertised — stretching on a hostel floor, watching the courtyard light up, sitting with tea and no screen while the world assembles itself.

Here is what the thirty minutes actually protect: the phone in the first hour is other people’s priorities wearing your alarm clock’s uniform. Somebody else’s deadline, somebody else’s opinion, somebody else’s morning — all arriving before you have said one word to yourself. The first hour is the only hour of the day that is truly yours by default. Give it to strangers and you have sold the day before breakfast.

5.3.4 The Hostel at Six

Nobody writes about the hostel at six in the morning, which is a shame, because it is a different building. The corridor holds a silence that daytime never affords. The one early friend shuffles past with his towel like a man carrying state secrets. From the window, the campus looks like a document nobody has edited yet — clean, possible, entirely yours. Even the canteen’s first chai, poured at seven, tastes like it was brewed for a secret society.

The early risers of any hostel form an unspoken fraternity. We never founded a club; there were no posters. But there was a nod exchanged in the corridor at six-fifteen, a quiet brotherhood of people who had chosen the same strange hour. The gym-going senior, the girl from the next wing who read on the steps, the watchman who seemed to know all our names by the first month. Mornings collected a small, silent tribe.

What that tribe shared was not discipline in the heroic sense. It was simpler: we had discovered that the day gives its best hours away for free before anyone starts demanding them. Mornings are the only time the world has not yet filed its tickets against your attention. Once you have tasted a morning you owned completely, eight-thirty starts to feel like arriving late to your own life.

5.3.5 The Weeks the System Fell

Now the honest page, because habit books that show only victories are advertisements, not memoirs. The morning system fell apart regularly. Exam weeks dismantled it. Hackathon nights — and my hackathon chapters are a long confession about those — wrecked it deliberately, one three a.m. shift at a time. When I flew back from Malaysia, my body clock filed for a formal separation and took a week to negotiate its return.

What saved the habit was not discipline; it was a recovery protocol. The rule I learnt somewhere in those collapsed weeks: never two broken mornings in a row. One bad morning is a data point; two in a row is the beginning of a new and much worse habit. So after every collapse, I forgave fast, restarted small — alarm across the room, water, nothing else — and let the system rebuild itself one morning at a time.

That is when I finally understood what a system is. It is not fragile glass that shatters when dropped; it is a habit that waits, patiently, for you to come back. The week you miss is not the system failing. The system includes the comeback. Engineers call it fault tolerance, and a morning routine, like any good service, should be judged not by its uptime alone but by how fast it recovers.

5.3.6 The Steering Wheel

The results of those owned mornings arrived without drama, which is how the best results behave. Lectures felt lighter when the day had already begun calmly. Errors in code looked smaller to a mind that was not racing from minute one. Even the mess queue felt shorter on owned mornings, which is either psychology or a genuine property of tea; I have never investigated, for fear of disproving it.

I am not a morning-motivation speaker — some of my proudest nights were sleepless ones, and this book has already honoured several of them. The claim I make is smaller and more useful: between two ordinary days, the morning is the steering wheel. On the days I held it first, the day went where I steered. On the days the phone held it, I spent the whole journey arguing with the driver.

So that is the whole system, and it fits on a small card: alarm across the room, water before the phone, thirty minutes that belong to nobody else, and a fast forgiveness protocol for the weeks it all collapses. No magic. Order, repeated until it becomes character. Whoever holds the morning first chooses the direction — and I would rather that somebody be me.

5.4.1 Whatever Is Available

We serviced our laptops with full sincerity in those years — updates, cleaning, careful charging, a cover bought with pocket money. Meanwhile the machine that actually ran our lives got whatever survived till midnight. In first year, my diet had a name, and the name was whatever is available. Extra salt, extra sugar, zero timetable. If existence was the only criterion, the diet passed with distinction.

An honest census of that year’s nutrition would make a doctor laugh and a mother cry. Maggi in at least two official flavours. Samosas at eleven at night, because the stall near the gate stayed honest when nothing else did. Chai functioning as a food group, mood stabiliser and social calendar simultaneously. And one family’s homemade parcel, rationed like state supplies because it was the only thing on the shelf with any retirement plan.

The strange part is that this diet worked — for a while. First-year bodies are forgiving machines; they run on anything and still carry you up three floors. But the body sends its invoices late, and with interest. The bill arrived in second year as headaches, dull evenings and a stamina graph that only went one direction. That was when I began treating food less like fuel stops and more like configuration files.

5.4.2 Water Before Chai

The change began, as the best changes do, embarrassingly small: water before chai. One glass, before the day’s first cup. That is the entire rule, and I am almost ashamed to report how well it worked. Hydration fixed half of what I had been diagnosing as tiredness, laziness or a bad attitude. My body, it turned out, was not demanding a new life; it was politely asking for a drink.

The chai census came next, and the numbers were grim. Six cups a day was my first-year average — one to wake, two to socialise, one to celebrate finishing work, one to mourn not starting, one because the canteen was simply there. I cut it to two without going to war: water first, chai after breakfast, chai at four. The first week produced withdrawal headaches I logged like a proper experiment. The second week produced something suspiciously like clarity.

Substitution, I discovered, beats prohibition every time. I never banned anything; I only made something better arrive first. Water before chai. Fruit before biscuits, when the canteen offered it. A refill of the bottle before a refill of the cup. Habits do not need to be executed; most of them only need to be outrun in the morning. The body takes its orders from whoever arrives first.

5.4.3 The Stomach’s Timetable

The next change sounds bureaucratic and worked like a miracle: I gave my stomach a timetable. One proper meal at roughly the same time daily, instead of five random snacks wandering the evening like unattended processes. The mess did half the work for me — its fixed timings were the first schedule in my life that I did not have to design, only attend. I began treating dinner at eight as a meeting I could not reschedule.

The results were absurdly quick. The body, it turns out, loves a schedule; within two weeks the hunger arrived on time like a well-maintained service, instead of ambushing me at midnight. Skipping breakfast — my old speciality, performed with pride — turned out to be a loan I was taking against my own afternoon, repaid with slowness and irritability. Regular meals were not a wellness theory; they were, quite literally, garbage collection running on schedule.

Of all people, engineers should appreciate this. We build systems that run best with predictable inputs and regular maintenance windows, and then we feed the human running them at random hours from a vending machine. The stomach is not asking for a diet chart; it is asking for a cron job. Feed it like a process you depend on, because you depend on it more than any code you will ever ship.

5.4.4 The Headache That Resigned

The first resignation letter I ever received came from a headache. For years, every long coding session ended the same way — a dull pressure behind the eyes at eleven, dismissed as the price of ambition. Then, some weeks into water, timings and actual meals, I noticed the pressure had stopped reporting for duty. Long sessions still ended, but in tiredness — the honest kind — instead of pain.

The other payoffs arrived quietly too. Evening games felt lighter, now that the body had fuel that burned steadily instead of spiking and crashing. My mood steadied like a server that has finally been given enough memory — I wrote that line in my diary that month, and I stand by it, because the machine was the same machine; only its resources had stopped being starved. Even focus stretched: two-hour sessions that used to feel like marathons began to feel like warm-ups.

That is when the connection became impossible to unsee. Focus, mood and stamina are not personality traits; they are downstream of the plate. The brain is a very expensive tenant and the stomach is its landlord, and you cannot stiff the landlord and expect the tenant to perform. Food is configuration for the mind. Change the configuration, and the same code runs differently.

5.4.5 Birthday Cakes and Cheat Weeks

Now the page the diet books never write honestly: my eating system survived because it allowed festivals. Every birthday in the wing meant cake at midnight. Every festival meant somebody’s mother had sent something fried and sacred. Every exam result, every hackathon win, every small happiness in hostel life arrived attached to food, and I refused to be the monk who photographed his plate while his friends celebrated.

So the rule was built to bend: enjoy fully, and return by the next meal. One day of feast never ruined anything; one week of treating a festival as a lifestyle did. The trick was planning the exceptions instead of being ambushed by them. A birthday is not a failure of discipline — it is the schedule working. The guilt, not the cake, was the only genuinely harmful ingredient in the whole arrangement.

This is the page where I lose the audience that wanted perfection, and I accept that trade. Hostels are not wellness retreats, and college is not a retreat of any kind. A perfect diet is impossible there; a better one is always one small choice away — less fried, more water, regular timings, and full permission to enjoy the party. Moderation is not the enemy of joy. Guilt is. Plan the exceptions, or they will plan you.

5.4.6 Garbage In, Garbage Out

Every programmer learns one law in the first month and spends the rest of the career watching it come true: garbage in, garbage out. Feed a system bad input and no amount of clever processing will save the output. What nobody told us was that the law does not stop at the computer. The body runs the exact same law, with longer compile times and a much stricter code review.

The laptop comparison followed me for years because it was too accurate to ignore. We updated our machines, cleaned their fans, replaced their batteries with ceremony. The machine that runs the eyes reading this page and the hands that wrote this book got whatever survived till midnight. We were, as a generation, servicing our laptops with more sincerity than our bodies — and then wondering why the humans kept lagging.

So this sub-chapter closes with the only nutrition advice I am qualified to give, and it is a programmer’s advice: feed the body like something you are building for the long term, because that is precisely what it is. There are no rewrites available for this system — no factory reset, no migration to a newer model. Only small commits, made daily, compounding in one direction or the other. Garbage in, garbage out. Water in, everything out.

5.5.1 The Report That Embarrassed Me

Here is an ironic confession from a programmer: the habit that saved my focus was spending less time on screens. In second year, scrolling through my phone’s settings one bored evening, I opened the screen-time report — the app built to show you exactly what you refuse to see. It presented my month in a neat bar chart, the way only software can present evidence of a crime.

I did some honest maths on that report. My daily average, collected across a month, totalled hours that could have built a complete project — with documentation, and time left over for a demo. The top three apps were not tools of my ambition; they were spectators of it. That was the sentence I wrote in my diary that night, and it has stayed accurate ever since: I was not using the phone. The phone was scheduling me.

The embarrassment lasted exactly as long as it takes to turn embarrassment into a plan. You cannot fix what you do not measure — the oldest law of engineering, and the oldest law of habits too. That report was the debugger for my attention: it did not judge me, it simply showed me the call stack of my evenings. Every fix I will describe in the next pages started from that one uncomfortable screenshot.

5.5.2 The One-Week Experiment

I treated the fix like an engineer treats any vague problem: as a one-week experiment with a clear design. Three variables, no more. Notifications off for everything except calls. The phone sleeps outside the study table, in a drawer that became famous in our room as the phone’s hostel. And one full screen-free hour daily — walking, stretching, or simply staring at the ceiling like a philosopher on a budget.

Then I made the mistake that made the experiment work: I announced it. My circle heard the plan and reacted with the warmth of a court hearing an appeal — you will not last two days, said one, quoting history as evidence. The bet was informal, but the audience was real. Group accountability, I have found, is cheaper than discipline and twice as effective, and my circle has never once let me forget a public promise.

Habit change, I have decided, should always be run this way — small, measured, reversible, and slightly public. A week is too short to be terrifying and long enough to produce data. Announcing it converts stubbornness into support. And framing it as an experiment removes the moral weight: I was not failing as a human; I was simply running version one and collecting feedback for version two.

5.5.3 Withdrawal Symptoms

Day one was humiliating. My hands kept reaching for a phone that was not on the table — the reach itself was automatic, the discovery of nothing there was a small grief, repeated dozens of times before lunch. I felt phantom vibrations in a pocket that had held nothing for hours. The first two days felt like my hands were missing an organ, and I logged each reach like a scientist logging symptoms.

The reflex reaches were the data that embarrassed me most. I counted them the second day: more than forty deliberate reaches toward a device that was locked in a drawer. Forty times a day, my hand proposed a task to my brain before my brain had requested one. The habit was not that I loved my phone. The habit was that my hand had been trained to check, and checking had become the body’s default state.

The discomfort, I realised by day two, was information — the exact measurement of how deep the habit had run. Nobody enjoys withdrawal symptoms, but they do prove the dependency honestly. I wrote the count on the wall next to my other experiments, and looking at that number did more persuading than any lecture on digital wellness ever could. Data, again, beat discipline.

5.5.4 The Return of Boredom

Day four delivered the guest I had not invited: boredom. Genuine, unfilled, old-fashioned boredom — the kind my childhood used to be made of. And behind boredom, quietly, ideas started lining up. A project I had postponed for months presented itself with a plan attached. An old bug’s solution surfaced while I watched the ceiling. A letter I owed someone wrote itself in my head, almost completely.

The mechanism, once seen, could not be unseen. The scroll was eating the empty gaps — the queue, the lift, the walk, the minutes between things — and those gaps were not dead time. They were where thinking used to live. Every queue, every walk, every ceiling I had ever stared at had been silently processing the day. The scroll was not stealing my free time. It was stealing my processing time.

By day four the experiment had stopped being about screens at all. It was about what returns when the noise stops: boredom, and behind boredom, ideas, patient as a queue that never complains. I understood then why my best ideas had always arrived in showers and bus rides and badminton rallies. Not because those moments were special, but because they were the last uncolonised gaps.

5.5.5 The Rules That Survived

Experiments, like products, must survive contact with reality, so let me report honestly what lived and what died. What survived: notifications by whitelist only, the phone sleeping outside the bedroom, and the one screen-free hour, which quietly became permanent. What died with honour: my grand ambition of a full digital detox weekend, which collapsed at noon on day one and taught me more than the victories did — ambition is a poor architect for habits; design is better.

The balance that finally worked surprised me with its simplicity. Screens were never the enemy — code is my favourite screen, and this entire career would be impossible without them. The enemy was the endless scroll: the feed designed to have no bottom, consuming exactly the minutes my mind used for its own housekeeping. The difference between a tool and a trap, I learnt, is whether you decide when it ends.

So I designed my screen life the way I would design any interface: fewer interruptions, clear exits, no infinite feeds in the default view. The phone that remains is more useful than the one I had before — it works for me the way tools should, and waits politely when I am busy thinking. An engineer’s habits, in the end, are just user interfaces for the self. Build yours deliberately, or somebody else’s feed will.

5.5.6 Using, Not Being Used

The one-week experiment taught me the difference I had been missing all along: the difference between using a device and being used by one. The test is simple and brutal — when you pick it up, did you decide, or did it? A tool answers to its user. A scheduler does not ask. For years my phone had been the latter, wearing the costume of the former.

The long-term changes arrived in places I had stopped measuring. Reading came back — actual books, with pages that do not refresh. Conversations with friends lengthened, because a pocket full of strangers had stopped interrupting the two people actually sitting there. Walks became interesting again, and not because the route changed; because nothing was competing with it. The world, it turns out, had been in high resolution the whole time.

Screens are not the enemy — I want to say that once more before this sub-chapter closes, because I love my screens. The enemy is the endless scroll that eats the empty gaps where thinking used to live. Guard those gaps fiercely, the way you would guard any production resource. Silence is not wasted time. It is where the next idea waits, patiently, behind all the noise.

5.6.1 The Burnout That Whispered

My first burnout never announced itself. There was no dramatic collapse, no week in bed, nothing worth a scene in a film. It simply made everything slower, flatter and heavier — sessions that once flew now dragged, small tasks felt like furniture to be moved, and weekends stopped refilling anything. I blamed the syllabus, the weather, the mess, the moon. Everything except the actual cause, which was that I had not rested in months.

In engineer’s terms, the symptoms were familiar from a machine I would have diagnosed instantly. Compile times grew longer for the same code. Motivation began throwing random segfaults. Small tasks, which had always run in the background, started blocking the main thread. Joy’s build failed silently on every branch. If a server behaved this way, I would have checked its resource usage before touching a single line of logic.

Instead I pushed harder, because that is what we are trained to do. Here is the truth I want this page to carry: burnout does not begin with a crash; it begins with a whisper. Flatter days. Slower mornings. Work that still gets done but costs triple. Learn the whisper — it is the cheapest consultation you will ever get, and the only one that arrives before the bill does.

5.6.2 Escaping Is Not Resting

Recovery taught me the distinction my generation most needs: the difference between escaping and resting. I had been resting, I believed, for hours every day — three hours of scrolling after work, videos until the screen blurred. But three hours of scrolling is not rest; it is simply a different screen. Escape changes the channel. Rest changes the state. I had been changing channels with a dead battery.

Real rest, when I finally tried some, turned out to be embarrassingly plain. Sleeping at a fixed time — the same hour, honestly kept. One guilt-free off day a week. Doing nothing without apologising for it, which proved harder than all the deadlines of the year combined. A walk without a destination, a meal without a screen, an evening that produced nothing and was therefore successful.

The hardest ingredient, by far, was the guilt. An engineering brain whispers that rest is a loss — an hour with no output is a defect, a leak, a waste. It took me embarrassingly long to see the flaw in that model: the body is not a machine that runs on output alone. It is a machine that also requires maintenance, and maintenance, as every engineer knows, is part of the work — not a tax on it.

5.6.3 Sleep Like a Deadline

These days I treat sleep like a deadline — fixed, respected, non-negotiable. The comparison makes my friends laugh, but it is the only framework my brain has ever obeyed without rebellion. A deadline does not negotiate, does not accept excuses, and does not extend itself because the work was interesting. Sleep, discovered late and kept since, works exactly the same way.

The mathematics I discovered still surprises people: seven hours of rested work regularly beats twelve hours of tired work. I have the receipts, from hackathons. The national finale taught it first — our shift system, two sleeping while two built, out-produced teams that glorified exhaustion (I wrote it then as stamina being a design decision, and I have never updated that line). Sleep debt, I have since understood, compounds exactly like technical debt: invisible on day one, catastrophic on day twenty.

There is no heroism in exhaustion; there is only postponed payment. The body will take its rest either way — the only choice you have is whether the stop is scheduled or unscheduled. Every system has a maintenance window. Yours is either the seven hours you planned, or the two days you did not. Sleep is not the reward for finishing the work. It is the infrastructure the work runs on.

5.6.4 The Off-Day Protocol

The guilt-free off day was the hardest rule to install and the easiest to defend once running. One day a week, fully off — the court in the evening, a long lunch with the circle, an afternoon nap that apologised to nobody, and not one line of productivity, planned or accidental. The first two off-days felt like crimes in progress. By the fourth, they had become the week’s load-bearing beam.

What surprised me was the economics. Output did not drop; it sharpened. Monday arrived carrying ideas that Sunday’s rest had apparently loaded overnight — the messy problems unsolved on Friday dissolved by Monday noon, without anyone touching them in between. The off day was not a pause in the work. It was a stage of the work, the way compilation is a stage of building: nothing visible happens, and nothing could happen without it.

So the protocol stands, and I recommend it with full confidence: one off day a week, protected like a production deploy. What it must contain — rest that refills, people who refill, at least one meal that behaves like a festival. What it must not contain — guilt, and the word just in the sentence I am just resting. There is no just. Rest is not the opposite of productivity. It is the fuel station productivity keeps visiting, whether you schedule the stop or not.

5.6.5 Rest for the Mind

The body’s rest was only half the syllabus; the mind needed its own, and it took me longer to learn. Mental rest has its own small catalogue, and none of it is impressive to watch: a walk without headphones, a meal without a screen, an evening with friends where nobody discusses work. Each of these is defragmentation for a machine nobody can see but everybody can hear when it is noisy.

Our circle invented its own restart buttons without ever calling them that. The evening court was defragmentation — forty minutes in which the mind’s open tabs closed themselves. The mess table at eight was cache clearing: the day got spoken through once, and then it stopped replaying. The two o’clock conversations were full restarts. None of this was labelled mental health. All of it was.

What I protect now is the mind’s idle time, and I defend it with the same seriousness as study hours, because idle is not idle. The idle mind is the only state in which it edits, connects and files the day’s work. A mind never rested does not think harder; it just thinks in loops. Every machine we build has an idle state, and we designed it in deliberately. Give your own machine the same courtesy.

5.6.6 Maintenance Is Part of the Work

The reframe that finally stuck, years after the first burnout: maintenance is part of the work, not a tax on it. No team runs a production server twenty-four-seven without maintenance windows and then acts shocked at the crash — we would call that negligence. Yet we run ourselves on exactly that schedule and call the crash a personal failure. The double standard is so old that we mistake it for maturity.

So the arithmetic I keep repeating, because it is the entire subject in one sentence: seven hours of rested work, regularly, beats twelve hours of tired work, occasionally. Rest is not the opposite of productivity. It is the fuel station that productivity keeps visiting — whether you schedule the stop or not. The choice is never between resting and working. It is between a planned stop and an emergency one.

For years I treated rest like a salary — finish the work, then earn the break. The flaw in that deal is simple: the work never finishes, so the break never arrives, and the body collects the payment later with interest. I have since renegotiated the contract. Rest now comes first, scheduled like the deadline it quietly is, and the work — I can report from both sides of the deal — has never been better.
